\title{Automating Legacy Equipment Register Migration: Duplicate Detection and Taxonomy Assignment at a Precision Floor}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Md Ashik Reza}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Equipment registers in higher education are commonly held in spreadsheets
that impose no constraints on what is recorded, so that the same item is
entered more than once and in more than one form. Replacing such a
spreadsheet with a governed register requires those records to be
reconciled, and it is this migration step, rather than the construction
of the system itself, that determines whether the resulting register can
be trusted. This paper studies how far that reconciliation can be
automated. Two corpora are combined: an established entity resolution
benchmark supplies human labelled duplicate pairs, and United Kingdom
Contracts Finder notices supply free text procurement descriptions
already carrying Common Procurement Vocabulary codes. A parameterised
degradation model reintroduces the error classes that published data
lacks, and lexical, embedding based and language model approaches are
compared for duplicate detection and for taxonomy assignment. Results are
reported not as headline accuracy but as the share of a register that can
be migrated without human intervention while precision is held at a fixed
target, together with the residual manual effort and the monetary cost of
each approach. At a 0.95 precision floor, a cost-aware cascade
automates 93.4\% of duplicate resolution on the benchmark corpus; exact
lexical features outperform sentence embeddings for taxonomy
assignment wherever labelled examples exist to learn from, and the
reverse holds for untrained retrieval; and transferring the pipeline to
a procurement-domain corpus shows the matching key transfers while the
blocking guard does not without an order-of-magnitude cost in candidate
volume. The resulting pipeline is deployed inside an asset
management system delivered to the Faculty of Computing, Engineering and
Science at the University of South Wales.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Higher education institutions hold research and teaching equipment worth
many millions of pounds, and the records describing that equipment are
very often kept in spreadsheets that were never designed to act as an
authoritative register. A spreadsheet offers no access control beyond
file permissions, no audit trail, and no constraint on what is typed
into it: two members of staff recording the same microscope in the same
week will produce two rows that differ in spelling, abbreviation,
capitalisation and column alignment, and the file will accept both
without complaint.

The Faculty of Computing, Engineering and Science (FCES) at the
University of South Wales is relocating a large volume of equipment into
a new building, Calon, and requires a live system in which any item can
be reached by scanning a QR or barcode label, updated in place, and
associated with photographs, service history, a floor plan position and
the relevant risk assessment, with tiered access across roles.

Building such a system is a well understood software engineering
exercise. Populating it is not. The migration step, in which thousands
of free text rows written by different people over many years must be
reconciled into a consistent structured register, is where asset
management projects most often lose accuracy. Duplicates that survive
migration overstate holdings and distort replacement planning; an item
placed in the wrong category inherits whatever hazard classification
and servicing rule that category carries, so a classification error
propagates directly into a safety control.

These consequences are not hypothetical. In 2024/25 employers in Great
Britain reported 59,219 non fatal injuries under the Reporting of
Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations, and the Health
and Safety Executive estimates the annual cost of work related ill
health at \pounds22.9 billion \cite{hse2025}; handling, lifting or
carrying and being struck by a moving object together accounted for 27
per cent of reported non fatal injuries \cite{hse2025}. Correct
identification of a machine and its risk assessment is a practical
control, not an administrative formality.

This paper therefore treats the migration problem itself as the object
of study. The question is not whether a live register can be built, but
how much of the transition from an uncontrolled spreadsheet to a
governed one can be automated, how accurate that automation is, and how
much human effort remains once it has been applied.

The study is carried out alongside delivery of that system, and the two
are related rather than parallel. The migration pipeline runs inside the
delivered system as a bulk import: records it resolves confidently enter
the register directly, and the remainder enter a review queue that
records the duration of each decision, so the system is both the
destination of the migration and the setting in which its residual
human cost is measured rather than assumed.

Records held by FCES were not available for this study, and published
institutional catalogues carry neither duplicate annotations nor a
consistent category label, so the study draws on two corpora with
complementary ground truth: an established entity resolution benchmark
supplies human labelled duplicate pairs against which results can be
positioned in the literature, and United Kingdom Contracts Finder
notices supply free text procurement descriptions already carrying
Common Procurement Vocabulary codes at a scale no manual annotation
could reach. A parameterised degradation model reintroduces the error
classes absent from both, so performance is observed as record quality
falls rather than at one arbitrary operating point.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item A two corpus evaluation design separating duplicate ground truth
from category ground truth, measuring an asset migration pipeline end
to end without access to a proprietary register.
\item A parameterised degradation model, extending the dirty data
protocol used in the entity matching literature, reproducing the error
classes documented in manually maintained asset and procurement data.
\item A comparative evaluation of lexical, embedding based and language
model approaches to duplicate detection and taxonomy assignment,
reported with accuracy, latency and cost.
\item A cost aware cascade in which inexpensive similarity measures
resolve confident matches and non matches while a language model
adjudicates only the ambiguous band.
\item An operating point analysis reporting the automatable share of a
register at a fixed precision target, the residual manual effort this
implies, and an implementation inside a system delivered to FCES.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions and Scope}

The study is organised around three research questions.

\textbf{RQ1.} How accurately can duplicate and near duplicate equipment
records be identified, and how does that accuracy degrade as the quality
of the underlying records falls?

Duplicate detection is treated first because every later stage depends
on it. A register containing the same item twice will apply the same
servicing rule twice and count the same asset twice. RQ1 compares
normalised exact matching, character level lexical similarity, sentence
embedding similarity and a hybrid cascade, measuring each across a range
of injected error rates rather than at a single operating point, so that
the sensitivity of each method to record quality is observed rather than
assumed.

\textbf{RQ2.} How accurately can free text equipment descriptions be
assigned to a controlled procurement taxonomy, and what accuracy, cost
and latency trade offs exist between classical, embedding based and
language model approaches?

Consistent categorisation is what makes a register searchable,
reportable and capable of carrying category level hazard and servicing
rules. RQ2 evaluates assignment to the Common Procurement Vocabulary,
which every UK public sector procurement notice already carries. The
vocabulary is hierarchical, and performance is reported at the two-digit
division and four-digit class levels separately, since a coarse but
reliable assignment has different practical value from a finer but less
certain one. The eight-digit leaf level is not evaluated: the label
distribution is too sparse at that depth for the result to carry
meaning, reported in Section V.

\textbf{RQ3.} What proportion of a legacy register can be migrated
without human intervention at a fixed precision target, and what
residual manual effort does the remainder represent?

RQ3 converts the measurements produced by RQ1 and RQ2 into the quantity
that determines whether the approach is adopted in practice. A method
that is accurate on average but cannot be operated at high precision is
of little use to a faculty that has to sign off a register, because
every error it introduces has to be found again by hand. Reporting the
automated share at a precision floor, rather than a headline accuracy
figure, states the result in the terms in which the decision to adopt or
reject the pipeline is actually made.

Three boundaries are placed on the scope of the work. The study
addresses migration of an existing register, not prediction of
equipment failure; servicing is a scheduling problem driven by category
and interval, and predictive maintenance is left to further work. The
location model is a two dimensional pin on an uploaded floor plan image
rather than a spatial or building information model, matching the
granularity at which the faculty records location. All corpora are
published open data and no personal or commercially sensitive
information is processed, so no ethical approval beyond the standard
faculty declaration was required.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Record Linkage and Entity Resolution}

The problem of deciding whether two records describe the same real world
entity has a formal treatment dating to the probabilistic framework of
Fellegi and Sunter \cite{fellegi1969}, which models the comparison of
record pairs as a decision problem with configurable error rates.
Subsequent work systematised the surrounding pipeline, in particular the
blocking step that reduces the quadratic comparison space to a tractable
candidate set, and the similarity measures applied within it
\cite{christen2012,papadakis2020}. The blocking schemes evaluated here are drawn from this line of work. This literature is largely evaluated on bibliographic and
commercial product data, and the question of how the methods behave on
institutional asset records has received little attention.

\subsection{Learned Approaches to Entity Matching}

Mudgal et al. \cite{mudgal2018} explored the design space of deep
learning architectures for entity matching and established the benchmark
suite on which most subsequent work reports. Their study also introduced
a protocol for constructing dirty variants of structured datasets by
relocating attribute values into a free text field, which is the closest
published analogue to the column misalignment observed in manually
maintained spreadsheets. Li et al. \cite{li2020ditto} showed that
pre-trained language models applied as cross encoders improve on these
architectures, and Narayan et al. \cite{narayan2022} reported that large
foundation models perform competitively on data wrangling tasks without
task specific training. These approaches assume a labelled training set
drawn from the same distribution as the deployment data, an assumption a faculty
migrating a single spreadsheet cannot satisfy.

\subsection{Cost and Efficiency of Language Model Pipelines}

Where a large model is invoked per record pair, pipeline cost scales
with the number of decisions made rather than with accuracy. Chen et
al. \cite{chen2023frugalgpt} showed that routing most queries to a
cheap model and escalating only the uncertain remainder to an expensive
one recovers most of the expensive model's accuracy at a fraction of
its cost; the cascade evaluated here applies that principle to pairwise
adjudication rather than single-query generation. Cost is rarely
reported alongside accuracy in the entity matching literature, so what
a given level of matching performance costs to obtain remains largely
open, and that is the question this design answers.

\subsection{Text Classification against Procurement Taxonomies}

Assigning free text descriptions to a hierarchical controlled vocabulary
is a long standing text classification problem, addressed classically
with sparse lexical features and linear classifiers and more recently
with dense sentence representations \cite{reimers2019}. Siciliani et al.
\cite{siciliani2023cpv} classify Italian tenders directly against the
Common Procurement Vocabulary, but as a single flat prediction; two
properties of the present task are not addressed there or elsewhere.
The vocabulary is hierarchical, so a coarse assignment may be reliable
where a fine one is not, and the assignment carries a safety
consequence, since a category determines which servicing and hazard
rules an item inherits, so the precision at which it is made matters
more than average accuracy. Neither arises when the same classification
is performed for procurement analytics, which is where most existing
work sits.

\subsection{Equipment Cataloguing and Asset Management}

Equipment cataloguing in higher education has been addressed
operationally rather than analytically. Open source catalogue software
such as Kit-Catalogue \cite{kitcatalogue} and aggregation services such
as the Equipment Data Service \cite{jisc} publish and share
institutional holdings, and asset management practice is codified in
ISO 55000 \cite{iso55000}. Data quality in engineering asset management
specifically, rather than in data cleaning generally \cite{rahm2000},
has its own literature: Lin et al. \cite{lin2008dataquality} propose a
framework for the technical, organisational and personal causes of
asset data quality problems, and Woodall et al.
\cite{woodall2015classifying} classify those problems empirically, but
neither addresses the transition from an uncontrolled source into the
register in the first place. That transition, and what it costs to
automate, is the omission this study addresses.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Study Design}

The study separates two concerns that are usually conflated: whether a
method works against ground truth a human has verified, and whether it
continues to work when record quality degrades. Two corpora are used,
each supplying verified ground truth for one of the two tasks, and a
degradation model is applied to both so that the second concern can be
measured on each.

Duplicate ground truth is supplied by established entity resolution
benchmarks in which candidate pairs have been manually labelled. Category
ground truth is supplied by public procurement notices, which carry a
taxonomy code assigned by the purchasing authority at the time of
publication. Neither corpus is drawn from an institutional asset
register. That substitution is the principal limitation of the design,
and its consequences are set out in Section VI rather than left
implicit.

\subsection{Corpus A: Duplicate Ground Truth}

Matching accuracy is measured on benchmarks from the entity resolution
literature in which candidate pairs have been manually labelled as
matching or non matching \cite{mudgal2018,koepcke2010}. The text heavy
Abt-Buy benchmark \cite{koepcke2010,leipzigdbs} is used, because at
least one of its attributes
contains long free text, distinguishing equipment descriptions from
purely structured records. Size, splits and positive rate are reported
in Table~\ref{tab:corpus}; the supplied training, validation and test
splits are used unchanged, so results remain comparable with the
literature.

Product catalogue entries share the structural characteristics of
equipment records: a short designation carrying brand and model
information, attached to a variable free text description written by
another party. Published results also exist for this benchmark, so
methods evaluated here can be positioned against the literature. The
limitation, that the domain is commercial rather than institutional, is
carried through to the threats to validity, and is why the in domain
comparison in Section~\ref{sec:external} is reported alongside the
benchmark figures rather than in place of them.

\subsection{Corpus B: Category Ground Truth}

Category assignment is evaluated on notices published through the UK
Contracts Finder service in Open Contracting Data Standard format,
containing public sector information licensed under the Open
Government Licence v3.0 \cite{contractsfinder}. Every notice carries at least one Common
Procurement Vocabulary code assigned by the purchasing authority
alongside a free text title and description.

The corpus is filtered to the eight CPV divisions relevant to
laboratory, engineering and computing equipment (30, 31, 32, 33, 38,
42, 43 and 44), so the label distribution resembles a faculty register
rather than public procurement as a whole; Table~\ref{tab:corpus}
reports the resulting size against the ten-division alternative and the
class-level support each retains. The CPV hierarchy is reconstructed
from the code and description pairs the notices themselves carry, with
parents derived by truncation, so division and class level evaluation
are supported from a single source with no external vocabulary file.

This corpus plays a second role. Its records are drawn from the same
descriptive register as equipment entries, so degraded variants serve
as the in domain material for the duplicate detection experiments, and
a filtered subset populates the delivered system with demonstration
data.

Labels assigned by purchasing authorities are not error free, and the
publisher documents known quality issues \cite{contractsfinder}. A
40-record hand-reviewed sample estimates the disagreement rate with the
published code at 12.5\% (Wilson 95\% CI 5.5--26.1\%). Reporting the
rate alone is not sufficient, because label noise does not act only as
a ceiling on measured accuracy: where mislabelling is systematic rather
than clerical, a classifier trained on those labels learns the error
and is scored correct for reproducing it, so measured accuracy
overstates what a user would experience. A ceiling makes a result
pessimistic; a learned and rewarded error makes it optimistic, and the
second is the more dangerous reading to leave uncorrected. Where the
review identifies a recurring mislabelling, the per-class results are
examined for evidence the classifiers reproduce it, reported with the
records concerned rather than inferred from the aggregate.

\subsection{Degradation Model}

Published data is cleaner than a working spreadsheet, so a degradation
model reintroduces the missing error classes. The model extends the
dirty data protocol of Mudgal et al. \cite{mudgal2018}, which relocates
attribute values into a free text field, with six further classes
drawn from the quality issues the Contracts Finder publisher documents in
the released data and from a manual audit of a sample of it: abbreviation
from a domain
lexicon, character level insertion, deletion, substitution and
transposition, inconsistent casing, whitespace perturbation, field
omission, and variation in the notation of units and voltages.

Each class is applied independently with a probability governed by a
single severity parameter, producing a family of corpora spanning
lightly to heavily degraded records. Duplicate pairs are generated by
producing a second degraded copy of a source record under an independent
draw from the same model, reproducing the case in which two members of
staff enter the same item without reference to one another. Near duplicate distractors are also generated by degrading records that
are similar but genuinely distinct. On the procurement corpus these are drawn from pairs
sharing a taxonomy class and exhibiting high title similarity while
arising from distinct procurement processes. Establishing distinctness is harder than it appears. The publisher mints
an identifier per notice and not per process, so two notices describing
one procurement carry different identifiers, and a rule keyed on them
admits genuine duplicates into the negative set. The published
procurement reference is a better signal but not a sufficient one, since
it frequently encodes the issuing organisation instead of the process,
and the buyer identifier does not reliably resolve to a single body.
Candidates are therefore filtered by reference and by buyer, and the
contamination remaining after each filtering stage is measured and
reported.

Residual contamination is not eliminated but estimated. A random sample
of the surviving candidates is adjudicated against a written protocol,
released with the code, and the rate is reported with a Wilson score
interval. Adjudication is performed by a language model and not by a
human annotator, which is a limitation and is treated as one: the
adjudicating model is not the model used in the matching cascade, so a
systematic bias would not appear on both sides of one comparison, but the
estimate carries no external validation and should be read as indicative
of magnitude rather than as a measured constant.

The measured rate is high enough to determine what this corpus can and
cannot support, and that determination is itself a result. Contamination
in a negative set leaves recall untouched, since recall is computed over
duplicates whose membership is known by construction, but it corrupts
precision directly: a matcher that correctly identifies a contaminated
pair as a duplicate is scored as having produced a false positive.
Precision and F1 are therefore not reported on the procurement corpus.
Recall, pair completeness and the behaviour of both under increasing
degradation are, and precision, F1 and the operating point of RQ3 are
carried by the benchmark corpus, whose labels were produced by other
parties and published.

This is stated as a finding and not as an omission. Constructing an in
domain hard negative set from published procurement data failed here, and
it failed for a reason that generalises: the publication model emits
several notices per procurement while supplying no identifier that
distinguishes a process from an organisation, so pairs that are textually
near identical and genuinely non-matching cannot be separated reliably
from pairs that are near identical because they describe one purchase.
Two successive rounds of rule refinement did not resolve it. Anyone
attempting the same construction on the same class of data should expect
the same obstacle. On the benchmark corpus distractors are drawn from pairs sharing
a leading token. Without them a
detector that cannot separate similar but distinct records will report
high recall for the wrong reason. The model parameters, the
abbreviation lexicon and the generation code are released so that every
corpus used here can be regenerated exactly.

\subsection{Duplicate Detection}

Four methods are compared in increasing order of cost. Normalised exact
matching, applied after case folding, whitespace collapsing and
punctuation removal, establishes the floor and quantifies how much of
the problem is trivially solvable. Lexical similarity uses character
level n-gram term frequency inverse document frequency vectors compared
by cosine similarity; character level features are preferred to
word level features because the dominant error classes operate within
words. Semantic similarity encodes the concatenated name and description
with a sentence embedding model \cite{reimers2019}, which is expected to
tolerate abbreviation and paraphrase better and character noise worse.

The fourth method is a cascade. Pairs above an upper similarity
threshold are accepted, pairs below a lower threshold are rejected, and
only the band between them is passed to a large language model for
pairwise adjudication. Both thresholds are selected on the development partition, at each
degradation severity independently, to satisfy the precision target of
RQ3 while minimising the size of the adjudicated band. Selecting once on
undegraded records and carrying the result across would not satisfy the
target on the data it is applied to, and is not done.

A threshold satisfies the target when the lower bound of a Wilson score
interval on its precision reaches the target, not when its point estimate
does. The distinction is not pedantic. A point estimate admits thresholds
whose precision rests on a handful of accepted pairs, and one estimated
from a single pair is not an estimate at all; such thresholds are
selected on the development partition and then fail on the held out data,
which is the behaviour a precision floor exists to prevent. Requiring
confidence rather than a favourable point estimate also scales the
evidence demanded with the strictness of the target, which a fixed
minimum on accepted pairs would not.

The target is not always attainable. Where no threshold on the base
similarity reaches the required precision, the upper threshold is
undefined, nothing is accepted without adjudication, and the band extends
to every pair not confidently rejected. This is reported, not avoided: the severity at which the target ceases to
be reachable by a base matcher alone is itself a result, and it bounds
what any pipeline of this shape can offer on records of that quality.

A distinction follows that the cascade's structure makes easy to miss.
The threshold guarantees the target only on the pairs it accepts without
adjudication. Pairs resolved by the language model carry no such
guarantee, since no threshold governs them, so the precision of the
cascade's combined output is not bounded below by the target and may fall
beneath it. Both quantities are therefore reported: the precision of the
auto-accepted portion, which the selection procedure constrains, and the
precision of the combined output, which it does not. Where the two
diverge the difference is attributable to the adjudicator, and the
severity at which the combined output first falls below the target is
reported as the point beyond which the pipeline cannot deliver the
guarantee it was designed around. The proportion of pairs reaching the language model, and the resulting
cost per thousand records, are reported alongside accuracy, because a
cascade that improves accuracy at unbounded cost is not a usable result.

The three methods above are evaluated across the full factorial of
degradation severities and repetitions on both corpora, since none of
them carries a marginal cost per decision. The cascade is evaluated on
the benchmark corpus only. Its result is a trade of precision against
cost, and precision is not reportable on the procurement corpus, so
adjudicating a band there would consume budget to measure nothing the
paper states. On the benchmark corpus each adjudication is a paid call,
so the cascade is evaluated at a reduced set of severity levels and a
single repetition, with every pair in the band adjudicated at each. This
trades the resolution of the cascade's degradation curve for exactness at
the points measured, which is the better trade when the alternative is an
estimate at every point. The severity levels retained span the range
measured for the other methods, so the cascade can be read against their
curves rather than in isolation.

All methods operate over candidate pairs produced by blocking rather
than the full comparison space. Three keying schemes are evaluated: sorted
character n-grams of the normalised title, which applies to both corpora;
a leading token key taking the first substantive token of the title,
which approximates a brand designation where the title carries one; and
the identifier of the publishing authority, which is available on the
procurement corpus only. Blocking is evaluated separately by pair completeness and reduction
ratio, since recall lost during blocking cannot be recovered downstream,
and the availability of each scheme differs between the two corpora,
which is reported alongside its performance. The scheme parameters are selected on the
benchmark development partition, subject to a stated floor on pair
completeness, and carried unchanged to the procurement corpus in keeping
with the transfer design; any loss of completeness that transfer incurs
is reported rather than recovered by refitting. Because completeness lost
in blocking bounds the recall of every method downstream of it, a second
configuration selected on the procurement corpus is also reported, so
that the comparison between matchers remains measurable in domain even
where the transferred configuration does not support it. The two are
reported separately and never combined: the first answers what transfers,
the second answers how the matchers rank.

\subsection{Category Assignment}

Three approaches are compared, one from each family and in ascending
order of cost per decision. A lexical baseline represents each record as
character level n-gram features and classifies with a linear support
vector machine. An embedding baseline encodes the record with a sentence
embedding model and classifies with a logistic regression head. A
retrieval augmented language model condition is given the full
supported label set, not a shortlist of it: measured before spending
quota, the embedding retriever recovers the true code in its top twelve
for only a minority of records at class level, so a shortlisted label
set would have made the condition's accuracy a measurement of the
retriever rather than the model. Shortlisting is retained instead for
the in context examples, where an unhelpful example costs accuracy and
an absent label forecloses it entirely; the nearest labelled examples
from the development partition are supplied with each record. Shortlist
recall at k is reported as a ceiling in its own right.

The comparison asks whether a language model given the labels already
available in Corpus B outperforms a classifier trained on the same
labels, which is the question facing anyone deciding how to categorise a
register. The two classical approaches are evaluated on the full test
partition; the language model condition on a stratified sample of it,
whose achieved size is stated with the results, and the classical
figures are reported on that same sample alongside their full-partition
figures so the sample can be seen to be representative.

The two levels present different difficulties: division level is small
and only moderately skewed, so the task is tractable but coarse; class
level is an order of magnitude larger with a long tail, most classes
too rare to learn. Class level evaluation is restricted on two grounds
reported apart. Labels below a stated minimum of training examples are
excluded as unlearnable, and records whose published code names a
division and assigns no class are excluded as carrying no class level
ground truth at all, since scoring them would ask whether a classifier
can predict that a buyer was vague. Each restriction's share is reported
with the results, so accuracy is read against the share of the register
it applies to. Records outside the scored set are not discarded from
the pipeline; they are routed to review, where both an unlearnable and
an absent category belong.

Macro averaged scores are the primary measure at both levels, weighted
averages are reported alongside them, and a per class breakdown is
given for the divisions carrying hazard or servicing rules in the
delivered system, where an error has an operational consequence.

\subsection{Partitioning}

Every corpus is partitioned before any tuning. A development partition
is used for threshold selection, prompt design and model fitting, and a
held out partition is used only for the final reported results. In
Corpus A the published train, validation and test splits are used
unchanged, so that results remain comparable with the literature. In
Corpus B notices are partitioned by publication period rather than at
random, so that near identical repeat notices from the same buyer do not
appear on both sides, and so that the split reflects the way a register
accumulates over time. Without these constraints the
reported figures would be optimistic for reasons unrelated to method
quality.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Duplicate detection is evaluated by precision, recall and F1 on the
benchmark corpus, reported separately at every severity level rather than
summarised, since the components behave differently as records degrade.
On the procurement corpus only recall is reported, for the reason given
in Section IV: its negative set carries measured contamination that
depresses precision independently of any matcher. Blocking is evaluated
by pair completeness and reduction ratio. Category assignment is
evaluated by macro and weighted F1 at both taxonomy levels, with
confusion matrices for the operationally significant classes.

The operating point of RQ3 is computed from the benchmark corpus
severity sweep, since it is defined at a precision target and precision
is measured there.

Operational measures are reported for every method: mean processing
latency per record, monetary cost per thousand records for any method invoking a language
model, costed at the published rate card recorded with the run, and the automated share, defined as the proportion of records the
pipeline resolves without human intervention while holding precision at
or above the target specified in RQ3.

The automated share is not one quantity. It comprises confident
acceptance and confident rejection, and the two carry asymmetric risk. A
wrong acceptance creates a false merge, which a subsequent reader can
see and undo, and which the precision floor is fitted to bound. A wrong
rejection lets a duplicate pass into the register unreviewed, where it is
indistinguishable from a genuine second item and inflates the recorded
holdings; nothing in the selection procedure bounds it, and no one ever
looks at it. That is the harm this paper opens by describing, so
automation that produces it cannot be reported as though it were free.

Both sides are therefore reported at every operating point: the precision
of the accepted set, and the number and proportion of true duplicates
discarded by confident rejection without reaching review. The second is reported in absolute terms as well as as a rate, since a
faculty deciding whether to adopt the pipeline is choosing how many
undetected duplicates to accept in exchange for how much review it
avoids, and that trade is legible in counts and not in percentages.

A floor stated as one number does not demand the same thing of the two
rules, and the asymmetry is a consequence of the class balance rather
than of the design. Duplicates are a small minority of candidate pairs,
so accepting every pair would yield a precision close to the positive
rate and a floor well above it demands a large lift. Rejecting every pair
would yield a purity close to one minus that rate, so the same floor
demands almost nothing of the reject rule: it is satisfied by performing
slightly better than discarding the entire corpus. The nominal symmetry
of a single floor therefore conceals a rule that binds tightly on one
side and barely at all on the other, and the duplicates lost to confident
rejection are the price of that slack. A design intended to bound the
loss directly would constrain the number of duplicates in the rejected
set, and not its purity.

The operating point consequently fixes a ceiling on recall before any
adjudication occurs. Only pairs that are accepted outright or that reach
the band remain reachable, so the duplicates discarded by confident
rejection cap what the pipeline can find however capable the adjudicator.
That ceiling is reported with every operating point and the measured
recall is reported against it, because the gap between them separates two
different failures: the shortfall below the ceiling is attributable to
the adjudicator, and the ceiling itself to the choice of floor. Where the
measured recall sits close to its ceiling, improving the adjudicator has
little left to recover and the leverage lies in the operating point
instead.

Residual manual effort is reported as the count and proportion of records
the pipeline routes to review, and total effort is expressed as that
count multiplied by a per record handling time and not fixed by a single
measured constant. No handling time is measured here. One observer's
reading speed would not generalise to the staff who would perform the
curation, and quoting it as though it did would put a precision on the
effort estimate that the evidence does not support. A reader holding a
figure for their own institution obtains their own total; a reader
without one still has the review volume, which is the quantity the
automated share determines. The review queue in the delivered system
records the duration of each decision, so the figure becomes measurable
in deployment without being asserted in advance. The precision target is
0.95, consistent with the floor at which the automated share and duplicate
loss are reported throughout Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{External Validation}
\label{sec:external}

Results are checked in two ways. First, the error distribution produced
by the degradation model is compared against the manual audit of the
Contracts Finder sample, and agreement or divergence in the relative
frequency of each error class is reported. A degradation model that
overrepresents an error class the methods handle easily would flatter
every subsequent result, so this comparison is a precondition for
interpreting the rest.

Second, the pipeline is tuned on Corpus A and then evaluated, without
further tuning, on degraded records from Corpus B. The comparison is
made on recall and pair completeness, which the contamination in Corpus
B's negative set does not affect. The thresholds selected on the Corpus
A development partition are carried across unchanged; no parameter is
refitted on Corpus B, and the transfer is reported as a single paired
comparison rather than as two independent results.

A blocking guard complicates a naive reading of that comparison. The
blocking scheme caps how large a single key's posting list may grow
before it is dropped, to bound candidate volume; Corpus B is an order
of magnitude larger and more repetitive than Corpus A, so the same
absolute cap binds far harder there, and a completeness gap measured
under one shared configuration conflates the guard saturating with the
matching key itself failing to transfer. A cap sweep separates the two:
each corpus is measured at several cap values, holding severity fixed,
so completeness recovered purely by rescaling the guard is attributable
to the guard and not to the key. Rescaling is not free, and the
candidate volume it costs is reported alongside completeness for
exactly that reason. Because Corpus B's positives are byte-identical at
severity 0, that severity alone cannot show whether the key transfers
under real textual variation; the rescaled cap is therefore also
measured at the lowest severity where Corpus B's positives genuinely
differ, and the resulting pair completeness is checked against the
distribution of shared grams between true duplicate pairs, since an
absolute overlap threshold meeting two different amounts of headroom is
a candidate explanation for any gap that survives rescaling.

Corpus A carries human labels but a commercial domain; Corpus B carries
a domain closer to institutional equipment but synthetic duplicate
labels. Neither is a substitute for a real asset register, and the
difference between the two figures is the best available estimate of
how far the reported performance would transfer. That difference is
reported whatever its size and direction, and it is the first item in
the threats to validity discussion.

\subsection{Integration into the Delivered System}

The pipeline is deployed inside the asset management system built for
FCES so that its behaviour can be observed under realistic use, with a
filtered subset of Corpus B standing in for the faculty register until
the real records become available. The
system provides item records with attached photographs and documents, a
QR label per item resolving to a persistent item URL, pin placement on
an uploaded floor plan image, service interval tracking with scheduled
reminders, tiered access comprising administrator, technician and read
only roles, and an audit log of changes.

Migration is exposed as a bulk import in which an uploaded spreadsheet
passes through the deduplication and classification stages and the
outcome is routed to one of two destinations. Records resolved above the
precision threshold are written directly to the register. Records below
it enter a review queue presenting the candidate decision, the competing
alternatives and the similarity evidence, so the residual effort reported
here corresponds to a task a curator can actually perform and not to an
abstract count of uncertain records. The queue records the duration of
each decision, which is what makes the per record handling time an
institution would need obtainable from its own use of the system.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Collection scripts, the degradation model, the annotation protocol and
experimental code are released in a public repository, with third party
corpora distributed as record identifiers and reconstruction scripts
rather than redistributed data, per each licence. Model versions,
prompt texts and random seeds are recorded with every run, and the
language model tier is open-weights, so it is reproducible independently
of any one provider's endpoint remaining available. The repository is
available at github.com/thabir303/FCES-Management-system.

\subsection{RQ1: Duplicate Detection}

Table~\ref{tab:abtbuy} reports the four Corpus A matchers. Normalised
exact matching never clears the Wilson-bound precision floor at any
severity: Abt-Buy's true pairs are two independent retailers'
descriptions of the same product, so byte-identical text essentially
never occurs even at severity 0, and the corpus behaves as a benchmark
of paraphrase rather than of typographical noise. This is read as
validating the corpus's difficulty, not as the matcher failing to find
a threshold that does not exist. Sentence embedding similarity has no
defined threshold at any severity either, on the same criterion.
Character n-gram TF-IDF is the only base matcher with a defined
threshold at all, at severity 0.0 only: precision 0.970, recall 0.155.
The cascade, adjudicating only the band between an upper and lower
threshold, reaches recall 0.553 at combined precision 0.983 (auto-accepted
precision 0.970) at the same severity -- a 3.6x recall lift over TF-IDF
alone at effectively unchanged precision, the return the adjudicated
band is designed to buy. At severity 0.15 the upper threshold is itself
undefined, so nothing is auto-accepted and the reported precision
(0.851) and recall (0.417) are the adjudicator's alone; auto-accepted
precision is reported as undefined rather than 0.0, since 0.0 would
read as a measured failure rather than an empty set. At severity 0.5,
an 800-pair stratified subsample with every pair in the band
adjudicated, both precision (0.250) and recall (0.023) collapse: this
is the point past which the cascade's shape stops helping, not a
different failure from the one degrading the base matchers.

\subsection{RQ2: Category Assignment}

Table~\ref{tab:classification} reports both classical conditions at
both taxonomy levels. TF-IDF beats the embedding baseline at both
division (macro F1 0.759 vs. 0.709) and class level (0.560 vs. 0.410),
and the gap widens at the finer level: exact tokens carry more of the
distinguishing signal than semantic similarity does once the label set
is large and the tail long. Class-level evaluation routes 25.8\% of
test records to review, on two grounds reported apart: 15.3\% carry no
class-level code at all (division-only publication, not a model
failure) and 10.6\% carry a class below the training-example floor.
The eight-digit leaf level is not evaluated at all: 26,413 records
resolve to only 1,593 distinct leaves, 87 of them with 50 or more
examples and 385 appearing exactly once, confirming the sparsity RQ2
sets aside that level for.

The retrieval-augmented language model condition sends the whole
supported label set rather than a shortlist of it, since the embedding
retriever recovers the true class-level code in its top 12 for only
68.2\% of records against the 74-code supported pool and 26.0\% against

label-share drift 11 points). With Wilson 95\% intervals:
\texttt{rag\_fewshot\_llm} 0.829 [0.687, 0.915] (macro F1 0.805),
\texttt{embedding\_logreg} 0.756 [0.607, 0.862] (0.674),

The run measures its own cost: 1,623 tokens/call, 123 calls/day, 2.43
days of quota to reach n=300 -- affordable in principle, not within
the single day available this session. Class level's larger label
listing raises the per-call cost further, so the roughly 135
calls/day estimated for it in advance was already optimistic, and it
remains not attempted for the same reason.

\subsection{RQ3: Operating Point}

At severity 0 and a 0.95 precision floor, 93.4\% of Corpus A's test
pairs are resolved without human intervention, at a cost: 84 of 206
true duplicates are discarded by confident rejection before reaching
the adjudicator, capping recall at 0.592 however capable the
adjudicator is on what remains. Raising the floor to 0.99 trades
automation for safety on both sides of the operating point at once:
automated share falls to 64.4\% and duplicates lost falls to 16.
Residual manual effort is the review volume this automated share
implies, reported as a count and a formula rather than converted to
hours by a handling time no author's own reading speed could stand in
for.

\subsection{External Validation}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports the cap-sweep attribution. At the
shipped 500-cap configuration, severity 0.0, pair completeness is 0.985
on Corpus A against 0.411 on Corpus B. Rescaling the cap recovers
completeness -- 0.982 uncapped -- but not for free: Corpus B's
candidate volume rises from 36,614 to 44,277,952, 1,209x, for a
condition where Corpus B's positives are byte-identical and therefore
cannot speak to whether the matching key itself transfers.

Severity 0.25 is the lowest severity where Corpus B's positives
genuinely differ from one another. At the rescaled cap there, pair
completeness is 0.248 on Corpus A against 0.441 on Corpus B --
reversed from severity 0.0, and this time at 90,883,051 candidates
against 28,102. A degradation-damage check measuring mean normalised
edit distance from source rules out the simplest confound: Corpus B is
damaged \emph{more} by the same severity (0.652 against 0.516), not
less, so the reversal is not an artefact of one corpus receiving an
easier version of the same treatment. A shared-gram check offers a
partial mechanism instead. Right at the blocking threshold the two
corpora look identical (2.4\% of Corpus A's true pairs sit at or below
it against 2.6\% of Corpus B's), but a few grams above the line the
shapes diverge: at twice the threshold, 34.0\% of Corpus A's pairs sit
within that margin against 19.7\% of Corpus B's. Corpus A's true pairs
are shaped closer to the edge of the blocking key, so the same
degradation-driven erosion crosses that edge for more of them, sooner
-- a real, measured, contributing factor, not a full derivation of the
gap's exact size.

\section{Discussion}

Blocking bounds everything that runs after it, and the bound is not
visible from the matcher comparison alone. At severity 0.5, the
shipped blocking configuration retains only 4.9\% of Corpus A's true
pairs before any matcher acts; the cascade's measured recall at that
severity, 2.3\%, sits below even that reduced ceiling. The shortfall
between the two is the adjudicator's, on the pairs blocking already
retained, but the ceiling itself belongs to blocking, and a matcher
comparison read without it credits or blames the wrong stage.

The transfer finding carries a price that a completeness figure alone
does not show. The blocking key transfers, in the sense that rescaling
the same absolute cap recovers Corpus B's pair completeness to within
measurement distance of Corpus A's. But the guard that was masking that
result cannot be rescaled for free: recovering completeness at severity
0.0 costs 1,209x the candidate volume, and the severity-0.25 cell
needed to show the key surviving real textual variation already sits at
90.9 million candidates on Corpus B alone. Blocking parameters that look
independent -- an absolute size cap and a corpus's own repetitiveness --
are coupled through corpus statistics, and that coupling is what fails
to transfer even where the key itself does.

The severity-0.25 reversal, where Corpus B's rescaled pair completeness
exceeds Corpus A's, is not an artefact of the degradation model treating
the two corpora unequally: the damage check shows Corpus B receiving
\emph{more} textual damage at the same severity, not less. The mechanism
the shared-gram check offers -- Corpus A's true pairs sitting closer to
the blocking threshold's edge to begin with, so the same erosion crosses
it for more of them -- is a real, measured, contributing factor, stated
as exactly that and not as a closed derivation of the gap's size.

The two error types the operating point trades against each other carry
asymmetric risk, and a single precision floor does not treat them
symmetrically despite naming one number. A wrong acceptance is a false
merge a subsequent reader can see and undo, and it is exactly what the
precision floor is fitted to bound. A wrong rejection leaves a duplicate
in the register indistinguishable from a genuine second item, unbounded
by the floor and unexamined by anyone: at severity 0 the 0.95 floor
already discards 84 of 206 true duplicates this way, sight unseen. The
asymmetry follows from class imbalance rather than from the design --
duplicates are a small minority of candidate pairs, so a floor that
demands a large lift from the accept rule is satisfied almost
automatically by the reject rule -- and it means a design meant to
bound duplicate loss directly would constrain the \emph{count} in the
rejected set, not its purity.

Label noise propagates into a measured result here rather than merely
capping one. Code 4431 collides two unrelated senses of "hardware," a
builders' ironmongery class and IT/AV equipment, in the published data.
Both classifiers reproduce the collision: TF-IDF predicts it for 78
test records and is scored correct on 28 of them, the embedding
classifier for 44 records and 17 correct, in both cases against
examples that read unambiguously as IT hardware rather than
ironmongery. A noise rate acting only as a ceiling would make results
pessimistic; a noise rate a classifier learns and is rewarded for
reproducing makes them optimistic instead, and the second is the
reading a register migration cannot afford to leave unflagged, because
the register would inherit the same collision on every future record
routed by the pattern rather than reviewed against it.

TF-IDF and embedding similarity trade places depending on what the task
supplies. TF-IDF beats the embedding classifier at both taxonomy
levels, and the gap widens at the class level (0.560 against 0.410
macro F1) where the label set is large and labelled examples for any
one class are scarce -- exact tokens carry the signal where there are
enough labelled examples to learn which tokens matter. Embedding
similarity wins the opposite comparison: as an \emph{untrained}
retriever matching a record against the taxonomy's own descriptions, it
recovers the true class-level code in its top 12 for 68.2\% of records
against TF-IDF's 43.2\% on the same 74-code pool, with no labelled
example informing either ranking. The two measurements point in
opposite directions and one explanation covers both: exact tokens win
where labels exist to learn from, and semantic similarity wins where
they do not.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} The threat that matters most is stated
first because every other finding in this paper is conditioned on it:
FCES's own register was never available for this study, so both
corpora are proxies, and no sentence in this paper claims a percentage
measured on FCES's actual holdings. Corpus B's negative set carries
42.0\% measured contamination, which is why it reports no precision or
F1 anywhere, on any matcher, at any severity. The contamination rate
and the CPV label-noise rate were measured under different protocols on
different samples and are not comparable to each other, and neither is
compared to the other anywhere in this paper. Corpus A's benchmark
domain is commercial rather than institutional, which the external
validation in Section~\ref{sec:external} addresses directly rather than
assuming away. Reported cost is notional at a stated rate card against
zero actual spend, and would change under a different pricing model or
provider; it is reported as measured, not projected forward.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper measured how much of the migration from an uncontrolled
spreadsheet to a governed asset register can be automated, at what
cost, and with how much human effort left over, rather than treating
that migration as preparatory work beneath a system's engineering. On
the benchmark corpus, a cost-aware cascade holding a 0.95 precision
floor resolves 93.4\% of true duplicate pairs without human review at
severity 0, at the price of 84 undetected duplicates in every 206 --
a trade the operating point makes visible rather than hides inside a
single accuracy figure. Category assignment favours exact lexical
features over sentence embeddings wherever labelled examples exist to
learn from, and the reverse wherever they do not. What transfers to a
procurement-domain corpus and what does not is itself measured rather
than assumed: the matching key transfers, the blocking guard does not
without a 1,209x cost in candidate volume, and a domain-shift reversal
at the one severity where the two corpora's text genuinely differs is
attributed to blocking geometry rather than left as an unexplained
anomaly.

What this paper did not measure is as informative as what it did.
FCES's own register was never available, so every figure here is a
proxy result and none is a claim about the faculty's actual holdings;
that substitution, and what it costs the study, is stated rather than
absorbed into the headline numbers. No handling time is measured,
because no single reading speed generalises to the staff who would
perform the curation; residual effort is reported as review volume,
convertible by a reader who holds their own institution's figure. The
pipeline studied here is deployed inside the asset management system
delivered to FCES, so the review queue it feeds is positioned to
supply that handling-time figure, and the transfer gap this paper
measured, from deployment rather than from a second proxy corpus.
